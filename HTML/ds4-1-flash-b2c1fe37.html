Method, and what a verdict means here

Every entry below follows the same shape: the position in her own words, the context in which it was said, a chart drawn from published data, and a fact check that separates what is verifiable from what is a judgement call. Where she was reported indirectly, that is stated. Where a figure is an order of magnitude rather than a precise count, the chart says so.

Supported

The substance of the claim holds up against published, independent data. Caveats are noted but do not overturn it.

Mixed

Directionally right, but overstated, selectively framed, or true only under one definition or one time window.

Contested

Credible expert or institutional disagreement exists on the facts themselves — not merely on the politics.

Not verifiable

The statement was made privately or reported second-hand, and she has declined to confirm or deny it.

Scope. This dossier covers political positions and public statements only. It deliberately excludes personal life, family, biography, and the internal affairs of any party or government. Where other officials appear, it is only because their objection is part of the factual record of how a position landed.

Bias note. Criticism is not evidence. Each entry reports criticism because it is part of the record, then tests the criticism against data with the same standard applied to the claim itself.

01

That European defence can be redesigned from the top down

Defence integration 1 – 8 September 2026 Wicklow · Brussels

The position: closing Europe's capability gaps requires a small, high-level body of former leaders empowered to make recommendations fast, rather than another round of member-state negotiation.

On 1 September 2026, at an informal meeting of EU defence ministers in Wicklow, Ireland, Kallas announced a high-level advisory group on European defence — informally likened to a “Draghi report for defence.” Former NATO Secretary-General Anders Fogh Rasmussen was expected to chair it; former defence ministers Jüri Luik and Florence Parly were among the anticipated members, with the United Kingdom and Ukraine expected to take part.

The formal launch was set for 7 September at Brussels' Bibliothèque Solvay, as a fireside chat with roughly 150 invitees. It was cancelled on the afternoon of 6 September — before the group's membership had even been officially confirmed. Germany, France and Italy jointly rejected the plan; Berlin's objections were reported as central. A German defence ministry spokesperson restated the underlying principle: defence and armaments policy are member-state responsibilities, and capability planning derives from NATO planning.

Why it is contested

The objection was to method as much as substance. Two critiques ran in parallel: that ministers were not informed before the initiative was announced, and that the group would duplicate work already under way in the defence portfolio of the European Commission. There is also a live institutional dispute about which part of the EU machinery controls the defence agenda at all.

02

That Russia should pay for the war with its own frozen billions

Frozen sovereign assets Throughout 2026 Brussels · various

The position: the roughly €210bn of Russian sovereign reserves immobilised in the EU should be treated as an instrument of reparations for Ukraine, not as property Russia is entitled to have returned on request.

A widely circulated quote that does not survive checking. The line that Kallas said Russia must “humiliate itself” to get its money back traces to a single Russian outlet headline of 8 June 2026. The word appears in the headline only; the article's own body reports her differently, and the same day's doorstep in Nicosia was reported in English by Interfax-Ukraine with no such language. What she actually said there was that talks would require Russia's own core interests and European security interests to be respected — a conditionality statement, not a demand for humiliation.

A genuine 2026 Kallas “disgrace” formulation does exist, and it points the other way: “We should not disgrace ourselves by initiating negotiations” — said of European states, not of Russia, and about who should be asking whom for talks. This dossier does not use the “humiliate” line anywhere.

The real story is more technical and more revealing. On 6 January 2026 the EU made the immobilisation of the reserves indefinite — and did it by qualified majority under Article 122 TFEU, rather than by the unanimous six-monthly renewal that had applied until then. Hungary and Slovakia voted against and called the procedure unlawful. Belgium, Bulgaria, Italy and Malta voted in favour but entered a formal statement that the decision “under no circumstances prejudges a decision on the possible use of the Russian immobilised assets,” which must be taken by the member states' leaders.

That distinction — immobilise versus use — is the whole dispute. A reparations loan using the assets as collateral was proposed in December 2025 and blocked, and the €90bn loan the EU eventually agreed was raised on the market against the EU budget instead, precisely so that Russian money would not be in the structure.

Why it is contested

The objection is not primarily political but legal and financial. Most of the stock sits at Euroclear in Belgium, which carries the operational and litigation exposure. Belgium's position has been consistent and explicit — that the scheme “entails consequential economic, financial and legal risks,” that it is “not acceptable to use the money and leave us alone facing the risks,” and, as of 2 September 2026, that “the reasons for our opposition have not magically vanished in the interim.” The counter-argument from twelve MEPs' letter and others is structural rather than persuasive: pool the assets into a new EU instrument that assumes the legal obligations collectively, so the risk is shared.

What has actually moved is much smaller than the argument. Windfall profits — the returns on the assets, not the assets — have generated about €8bn since 2022, delivered in tranches, the fifth of which was €1.4bn in August 2026, and only a small share of that has gone to military aid. Her June 2026 announcement on this file was €80m. The gap between the size of the debate and the size of the transfers is the most striking number in the entry.

03

The comparison she would not confirm

Israel · Gaza 20 May – 18 June 2026 Mexico City · Brussels

The position, as reported: that Israel's treatment of Palestinians meets a description senior EU officials have historically avoided applying — the comparison with apartheid South Africa.

The origin is specific and worth stating precisely, because most coverage blurred it. The comparison was not made in a speech or an interview. It was reported by Euractiv as having been made in closed-door meetings with Mexican officials during an EU delegation visit to Mexico on 20–22 May 2026, reportedly referencing an earlier visit to the Apartheid Museum in Johannesburg. There is no verbatim public quotation. Her office neither confirmed nor denied it.

On 18 June 2026, Israel's foreign minister said he had “no choice but to sever all contact” with her, stating that she had issued “no denial, clarification, or response” about remarks made during the Mexico visit, and that the cutoff would hold “until she retracts the blood libel.”

Kallas replied on X without addressing the substance. She said she valued “dialogue and engagement” with Israel and that the EU remained committed to a “constructive relationship”, adding: “To bring peace to the Middle East, the Two-State Solution remains the only viable path. The EU has condemned the illegal Israeli settlements in the West Bank that make it increasingly difficult to get to that goal. That is the EU position.” The reply was answered with the same demand: stand behind the statements, or deny them.

Why it is the most consequential entry here

A High Representative's choice of vocabulary is itself an instrument of policy. Using the term raises the legal and political stakes of the EU's own instruments — above all the human-rights clause in its association agreement with Israel — and it forces capitals that prefer ambiguity to pick a side. The controversy is therefore not about whether she is entitled to the view but about whether the EU's top diplomat can hold it without the institution being bound by it. The non-answer is what makes the episode unusual: it leaves the political cost intact and the formal position unmoved.

Evidentiary caution. This entry is marked not verifiable on purpose. No verbatim quotation exists in the public record. The reporting is consistent and specific about where and when the remark was allegedly made, but the primary statement was private and she has neither confirmed nor retracted it. The chart below therefore maps the trade and diplomatic facts that surround the dispute — not the disputed sentence itself.

What is measurable is the gap between the dispute and the instruments. The EU is Israel's largest trading partner, accounting for close to a third of Israel's goods trade, and that entire relationship runs through a single legal instrument. The measures actually on the table — a ban on settlement exports, a cultural programme freeze — are economically tiny beside it. In July 2026 a move to ban trade with the settlements was delayed after member states failed to reach consensus. Kallas told reporters that banning trade with illegal settlements had gathered the most support, and that all 27 states agree the settlements are illegal under international law — while the Commission maintained that sanctions require unanimity and she maintained that a qualified majority suffices.

That disagreement is the reason this entry belongs next to the enlargement argument in section 05: the same procedural question decides both.

How it ended the year: the pressure moved around her

The clearest measure of what her position achieved is that the EU-level instrument never arrived, and national ones did. In July 2026 the settlement-trade ban stalled. Then on 8–9 September 2026, foreign ministers of twelve countries — Canada, Denmark, Finland, France, Iceland, Ireland, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Spain, Sweden and the United Kingdom — issued a joint statement announcing national restrictions, support for European measures, or active consideration of steps. Several went further: the UK signalled that it now considered Israel's occupation of Palestinian territories unlawful and moved to ban settlement goods; Spain had already prohibited settlement imports; Belgium had approved draft rules; Ireland had approved draft legislation.

The trigger was Israel's advancement of the E1 settlement project in August 2026, with 1,234 housing units tendered. Israel retaliated by announcing the closure of the British Consulate General in East Jerusalem. This is the outcome her argument predicted and could not deliver: with the unanimity route blocked, the same policy was executed by coalitions of the willing instead — which is both a vindication of her diagnosis and an illustration of the limit she spent 2026 arguing against.

Context, with attribution. The humanitarian backdrop to the dispute, as reported: Gaza's health ministry recorded 73,662 killed and 174,627 injured since October 2023, including 1,355 killed since the ceasefire took effect on 10 October 2025, according to its statement of 8 September 2026. The ministry is Hamas-run and does not distinguish combatants from civilians; wire services carrying the figure note that casualties remain trapped under rubble beyond the reach of ambulance and civil-defence crews. It is included here because it is the factual context against which the vocabulary dispute was conducted, and it is attributed rather than adopted.

04

That China holds leverage it is choosing not to use

China · Iran · Russia 2026 Brussels

The position: China is not a bystander to Russia's war or to the Middle East crisis. It is a patron whose restraint is a choice, and the EU should say so rather than compartmentalise trade from security.

The clearest version of this is not about Russia at all. In Estonia on 17 May she described Europe as understanding the “diagnosis” of its economic condition but not the treatment, and framed the choice as more “morphine” — subsidies to European industry — or “chemotherapy”: foreign-direct-investment screening, procurement rules, and diversification of critical raw materials. “Using these tools will not be easy,” she said, “because then there will be retaliation.” She added that she feared rich countries would eventually run out of taxpayers' money for subsidies “and we will not have solved the underlying problem.”

What happened next is the most consequential part. The European Commission publicly disowned the remark — its chief spokesperson stating that it was not the Commission's position — and five member-state governments complained to Brussels, according to Politico. An unnamed European official put the objection in one line: “You cannot talk like that as a diplomat.” Euronews subsequently published a fact check under the title “Viral posts distort Kaja Kallas' remarks on China and Russia,” and the Russian-language edition was slugged “Kallas is not a warmonger.”

The distortion runs in both directions, which is why this entry is marked mixed. What she said was a metaphor about Europe's own economic condition, applied to a trading relationship. What circulated was that she had called China a cancer. The metaphor is hers, and it is the reason the row happened; the noun is not settled across the languages it travelled through.

A note on the quotation. The widely circulated line that “China must convince Russia to stop and work to reopen Hormuz to Iran” is a condensed agency headline, not a verbatim sentence. The sourced doorstep remarks of 18 May 2026 point a different way: she welcomed Chinese pressure for free navigation at Hormuz, said the first phase had to be stopping the attacks and reopening the strait, and warned that accepting Iran's conditions for passage would “legitimise the blocking of other straits.” Read literally, the headline inverts her position. This dossier uses the sourced version.

The claim has two halves, and they have different evidentiary weights. That China provides material support to Russia's defence industrial base is documented and sanctioned — the twentieth EU sanctions package extended designations to entities in China, Hong Kong, the UAE, Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan and Belarus, and added Chinese firms to its export-restricted list. That China could stop the war if it chose to is an inference — influential, widely repeated, and not directly testable.

Where the China claim went further than the evidence

On 16 June 2026, at the Foreign Affairs Council press conference, she went past the documented supply relationship to a specific operational allegation: that the EU had “verified reports that Chinese military officers have been training Russian military personnel,” and that “Beijing remains a decisive enabler of Russia's war against Ukraine.” No further detail or evidence was offered.

NATO's Secretary-General declined to confirm the training claim the following day, endorsing only the general enabling picture — dual-use goods, sanctions evasion, support alongside North Korea and Iran. China's foreign ministry called the allegation “pure slander and defamation.” The general proposition is well supported: EU sanctions packages have repeatedly listed Chinese entities, including drone and shadow-fleet suppliers, and the twenty-first package extended restrictions to further Chinese firms. The specific claim about officers training Russian troops, which is the one that made headlines, remains unverified by any source outside the statement itself. That distinction — a documented pattern plus an undocumented particular — is the recurring shape of her China argument.

Her record on China is also less one-directional than the controversy suggests. Six weeks before the “chemotherapy” press conference, on 2 April 2026, she spoke by phone with China's foreign minister at the EU's request and, according to the Chinese foreign ministry's readout of the call, said the EU regards China as an important partner and does not seek to decouple from it. Both statements are hers. Quoting either without the other misrepresents her position.

Why it is contested

The objection is about means, not diagnosis. If China's leverage is as great as the claim implies, then the EU's own instruments — tariffs on electric vehicles, export controls on critical raw materials, de-risking — look small relative to the demand being made. Critics argue the rhetoric raises an expectation the EU has no way to enforce; supporters argue that naming the dependency is the precondition for the de-risking agenda she has pursued. Her own formulation concedes the difficulty: she said the tools would invite retaliation, and that Europe had not yet reached the goal.

The trade numbers cut both ways, which is what makes this entry genuinely mixed rather than merely critical. The EU ran a goods deficit with China of €359.8 billion in 2025 — exports of €199.6 billion against imports of €559.4 billion — and the imbalance widened again through the first half of 2026. A union that imports three euros of goods for every one it exports is not obviously holding the stronger hand when it demands that the other side exercise restraint.

05

That unanimity is the obstacle, and enlargement the answer

Enlargement · treaty reform 2026 Brussels

The position: the EU cannot make foreign policy at the speed the decade demands while a single capital can veto it, and the accession of Ukraine and others should be treated as a security commitment with a date attached, not an open-ended technical process.

This is the structural argument that sits underneath almost every other dispute in this dossier. If asset seizure, sanctions and defence integration can be blocked by one member state, then each of those fights is also a fight about voting rules.

On 22 April 2026, addressing a conference on enlargement at the European Parliament, she framed the question as a “geopolitical decision” and said she wanted new members inside the Union by 2030 so that Europe would be “safer, stronger and more stable.” She warned that enlargement could not be treated as a purely technical process but was a “strategic response” to hybrid attacks, disinformation and pressure on Europe's borders — and that the window of opportunity now open could narrow again. Georgia was her example of what happens when a country is left alone in the grey zone.

Why it is contested

Changing the voting rules for foreign policy requires treaty change, which requires unanimity — the very thing being challenged. Several capitals treat unanimity not as a procedural defect but as the guarantee that their foreign policy cannot be made for them. On enlargement, the objection is sequencing: that admitting members before they meet the criteria imports instability rather than exporting it. And the veto is not hypothetical — in April 2026 the twentieth sanctions package finally moved only once a prolonged deadlock was broken, and the €90 billion loan for Ukraine had to be structured around the same constraint.

Public opinion complicates her case in both directions. There is majority support for a common defence policy and for backing Ukraine; there is also a clear plurality that insists on the full merit-based accession process rather than a fast track.

She has stated the constraint more precisely than her critics do

The strongest evidence for her procedural argument is her own account of being bound by it. In the European Parliament on 19 May 2026, explaining why the EU could not suspend its association agreement with Israel, she said: “Suspension requires unanimity among member states. Unanimity does not exist. Qualified majority does not exist either.” Asked which states were blocking, she declined to name them — “I'm not giving you the list of these member states, because it's not good practice” — and conceded that “it's clear that not everybody's on board.”

That is a verifiable description of the machine she operates, and it is the same machine the chart below measures. But the count also cuts against the remedy: at an informal meeting of foreign ministers in Ireland in September 2026, only eleven of twenty-seven member states backed moving foreign policy to qualified majority voting at all — with Germany and France among the eleven. On this evidence the obstacle is not merely the rule; it is that the votes for changing the rule are not there.

Note also what she has not argued. Despite the framing often attached to her, no 2026 call by Kallas for formal treaty change was found in the record. Her position is consistently that the existing treaties settle the division of competences, and that the practical work has to happen within them.

On Ukraine's accession, the record contradicted her own timetable

On 11 May 2026 she set a public test for her own argument: “There is now a new momentum, and we must use it to advance Ukraine's path to the EU. This means opening all negotiation clusters by summer. Ukraine's accession to the EU is not charity. It is an investment in our own security.”

Two of six clusters opened — Fundamentals on 15 June and External Relations on 14 July 2026. Screening results for the internal market and competitiveness clusters were postponed at working-party level on 22 July, then blocked again on 1 September and a third time on 8 September, by a single member state demanding legislative steps on a national minority question. This is the sharpest illustration in the whole dossier of the argument she makes: the instrument was not slow because the EU lacked a plan, and it was not slow because of the technical merits — it stopped at one capital's veto, which is exactly the mechanism she spent 2026 arguing about.

The reform arithmetic underneath the timetable is thinner than the rhetoric. Independent civil-society assessments put implementation of the agreed priority reform roadmap at roughly 15%, and delivery against interim obligations on judiciary and fundamental rights at no more than about 3%. Montenegro has opened all 33 chapters and closed 16; Albania has opened all 33. Ukraine, on this evidence, had begun.

The Georgia case: a position that cost rather than earned support

The clearest example of her enlargement argument producing a backlash rather than a coalition is Georgia. On 22 April 2026, before the Foreign Affairs Council in Luxembourg, she said the EU “supports the people of Georgia, but not its government,” describing repression against the opposition, media restrictions and legislative changes as unacceptable. She had used Georgia earlier the same day as her illustration of what happens when a country is left in the grey zone.

Georgia's parliamentary speaker responded publicly on 13 July 2026, calling the formula a slight on Georgian voters: “This is not support for the Georgian people; it is disrespect for their democratic choice,” and warning that “where there is an attack, there will also be a response.” The substance of his objection is not that her facts about Georgian governance were wrong, but that the distinction between a people and their government cannot be drawn by an outside institution without denying the people's own agency — which is precisely the tension in treating enlargement as a security instrument rather than a democratic process.

06

That Europe cannot keep outsourcing its own defence

Rearmament · NATO 2026 Brussels · various

The position: Europe has been a consumer of American security rather than a producer of its own, and the correction is not a matter of choosing between NATO and the EU but of spending enough that the choice stops being existential.

This is the position of hers with the widest nominal support and the sharpest practical resistance. Almost no government disputes the direction. They dispute the timeline, the financing, and whether the money should be raised jointly — because joint borrowing socialises the cost and the debt.

On financing she has been explicit, backing joint European debt for defence on the argument that common borrowing makes loans cheaper for everyone, and pointing out that the EU already borrowed jointly during the pandemic. On structures she has been equally explicit — and significantly more cautious than her critics suggest. She has said that defence “has always been and remains the responsibility of EU member states,” that a single European army is not on the table because it is not possible for every member to raise another army under a European command, and that the goal is a distinct European pillar within NATO.

The frustration is not with the argument. It is with the output.

In Brussels on 12 May 2026 she said something unusual for a politician making this case: that she shared the disappointment. “It seems that industry is not ramping up production the way we expected.” Her diagnosis was fragmentation rather than money — different rules in every member state, standards tweaked just enough that every order is bespoke, and therefore no true mass production. On 1 September 2026 she added the line that undercuts the spending debate entirely: “Spending alone is not automatically a guarantee for success.”

The scale of what is being attempted, meanwhile, is genuinely large. On the European Defence Agency's figures, EU-27 defence spending reached €418bn in 2025 — up a fifth on the previous year and about 2.2% of EU GDP — and is projected at €454bn in 2026, against a Commission estimate that roughly €3.4 trillion is needed over the coming decade. SIPRI's parallel series records the largest regional increase in military spending anywhere in the world, with European expenditure up 14% in a single year. The bloc has set itself targets of 40% of military procurement being collaborative by 2027 and 60% by 2030, with the stated aim of closing all identified capability shortfalls by 2030 — the “readiness 2030” framing she invokes.

07

That sanctions are draining the Kremlin

Sanctions · Russia 2026 Brussels

The position: sanctions are working, the pressure is cumulative, and the correct response to slow results is more sanctions rather than a reassessment of the instrument.

The position has a second half that is often overlooked because it is about refusal rather than action. In March 2026, rejecting a call to normalise trade with Moscow in exchange for cheap Russian energy, she said: “If we go back to business as usual, we'll have more wars,” adding that “we must not give Russia what it wants because appetites will only grow,” and that member states did not want an energy deal with Russia “even behind closed doors.” That is the clearest statement of the theory underneath the whole sanctions architecture: that the instrument is not only coercive but preventative, and that its value includes the trade that does not happen.

In Lefkosia on 8 June 2026, after an informal meeting of EU defence ministers, she put a number on it: Western sanctions had already cost Moscow an estimated $1.2–1.5 trillion. She also said her services had proposed more than 80 new targets for the coming package, aimed at the Russian defence sector, human-rights abusers and state propagandists. It is a good example of her method — a vivid aggregate, delivered as evidence that the instrument is working.

Why it is contested

Because “draining” is not a number, and the number she uses is a stock, not a rate. Independent assessments generally find that sanctions have degraded Russia's long-run growth and its access to technology while failing to prevent it from financing the war in the short run. Both findings can be true at once, and which one you emphasise determines whether the policy looks like it is working.

The cadence tells its own story. Twenty-one packages in five years looks like relentless pressure — and it is also a record of how often the instrument had to be renegotiated. The twentieth package moved on 23 April 2026 only once a prolonged deadlock was broken; the twenty-first followed on 23 July 2026 after a further week's delay over member-state differences, and its most consequential energy measure, a ban on LNG terminal services, does not take effect until 1 January 2027.

Her fullest statement of the case came at the informal defence ministers' meeting in Wicklow on 1 September 2026: “Militarily, Ukraine is putting Russia under pressure. Ukrainian drones are disrupting Russian logistics and striking refineries deep inside Russia, causing the biggest fuel crisis in Russia in decades. Russia is losing well over 30,000 soldiers per month too. Overall, it has been a rough summer for Putin.” It is a compact example of her method — four claims in three sentences, of which two are well evidenced, one is definition-dependent, and one is a characterisation.

What that package had to leave out is the more informative record. Fish import bans were dropped even though Moscow earns more than $500m a year from EU sales. The listing of Patriarch Kirill was removed. Restrictions on former combatants were diluted by France and Italy. Greece resisted a ban on shipping LNG to third countries and Austria intervened over a bank's claim to €2.44bn it says was expropriated in Russia. As one EU diplomat put it: “There's no more low-hanging fruit, with 20 packages done. You'll bump into individual member state interests more.”

Her response has been to widen the net rather than to argue about the pace. She has said that for autumn 2026 she is putting forward the most far-reaching listings since the war began — some 1,600 entries for Russia's military complex — which she says would raise the total number of sanctioned Russian entities by a third. That is the same instinct as the asset argument and the defence group: when the instrument is slow, make it bigger.

The one claim of hers that was flatly contradicted

In Cyprus on 28 May 2026, after the informal foreign affairs council, Kallas praised the embassies that had stayed in Kyiv: “all the embassies stayed except one. So that also takes courage from those embassies. But yes, all the Europeans stayed. America left.” Ukraine's foreign ministry spokesperson contradicted it the same day, on the record: reports of the US embassy's departure were not true. Of everything in this dossier, this is the cleanest verified error — a factual claim about a third party, stated from a podium, corrected by the government concerned within hours. It sits here rather than in a section of its own because it is the same mechanism as the sanctions argument: a vivid, morale-raising formulation delivered with more confidence than the underlying fact supported.

Her casualty figures need the same treatment, and the reason is definitional rather than political. On 1 September 2026 in Wicklow she said Russia was “losing well over 30,000 soldiers per month.” Whether that is right depends entirely on what is being counted, and the honest answer is that it lands in the middle of a wide range rather than outside it:

  • Confirmed deaths only: Mediazona and the BBC Russian Service had verified 239,354 named Russian deaths by 13 August 2026, with confirmed deaths above 200 a day for two years. That is roughly 6,000 a month — well below her figure.
  • Irreversible losses: a Meduza investigation in February 2026 concluded battlefield deaths likely did not exceed 600 a day, and that adding the severely wounded who cannot return gives about 900 a day, or some 27,000 a month. Her 30,000 sits just above this.
  • Killed and wounded combined: ISW's monthly totals for 2026 run higher — 31,960 in March, 42,860 in July, 42,020 in August. On this definition her figure understates the case.
  • Official government estimates: British signals intelligence put Russian military deaths at “almost half a million” in May 2026 — the highest on-the-record figure from any government.

So the claim is defensible on one definition, conservative on another, and roughly double the confirmed-death count on a third. Her accompanying claim — that recruitment cannot keep pace — is independently supported: one research institute found Russia did not recruit more than 30,000 a month in 2026 and fell to 24,000 early in the year. Her “1.2 million victims” from February 2026, by contrast, remains unverifiable: no independent body publishes that metric, and it is not defined.

The same discipline applies to the economic claim, and it splits in two. “Russia is broken, its economy is destroyed” — her Munich formulation of 15 February 2026 — is contradicted: every independent forecast for 2026 is positive, and the IMF attributes even that modest growth to stronger commodity export revenues, which is the opposite of collapse. But a softer version survives easily, because the fiscal picture underneath the growth is genuinely deteriorating.

08

Method as message: announce first, negotiate later

Style · institutional method 2026 Brussels

The position, implicit in almost everything above: that public pressure is a tool of EU diplomacy, and that announcing a direction before securing consensus is a way of creating the consensus.

This is the most revealing criticism in the dossier because it is not about any single policy. It is the claim that the method itself is the problem: that initiatives arrive as announcements rather than proposals, that ministers learn of them from the press, and that the resulting collisions consume the credibility needed to make the next initiative succeed.

Eunews put it directly in September 2026: it is both the method and the substance of her work that are being criticised and contested, and by failing to inform ministers in advance she was repeating past mistakes — provoking vetoes and criticism in the process.

Testing the criticism rather than repeating it

The criticism is itself a claim, and it can be tested against the record. The pattern of 2026 is not that her initiatives fail — several succeed. It is that the ones requiring unanimous consent fail, while the ones requiring only a coalition, or only her own service's action, proceed. That is a structural pattern, and it is consistent with the criticism without proving the motive behind it.

The sharpest version of the critique surfaced in June 2026, when an informal French government paper floated three options for restructuring the role of High Representative — one strengthening it, two transferring its powers to the Commission or to member states. Her response was an internal note to staff restating that “the roles and responsibilities of the EU institutions are clearly defined in the treaties. That framework remains unchanged.” France subsequently softened the paper to an exploratory note. The charges it reflected are worth stating plainly, because they are the ones her capitals actually make: that she advances positions going beyond the consensus of the 27, that she tables ambitious plans without consulting first, and that she still behaves as she did in national office rather than as a broker of 27 interests.

One senior diplomat's assessment, reported in June 2026, is blunter than anything in the policy files: “She does, unfortunately, a lot to lose support. She is not very good at building alliances in the Council.” The counterweight is the coalition that does back her, and it is consistent: the Nordic and Baltic states and Poland. Where the paper was aimed — France — the same reporting notes that Paris was, at the time, in “all-attack mode against all institutions,” which is its own caution against reading the criticism as being only about her.

What the criticism is actually about — and what it is not

It is worth being precise, because the vocabulary used about her is often looser than the evidence. The documented 2026 criticism is about process, ownership and duplication: that ministries learned of initiatives from the press, that a body attached to her own service would take agenda-setting away from member states, and that it overlapped work already under way elsewhere. That is the substance of the German position and of the commentary that followed the collapse — including one German commentary's conclusion that her competences had been exceeded because “defence falls within the competence of the EU states,” and its closing line that around her it had “become lonelier still.”

An editorial label, not a quotation. The phrases “megaphone diplomacy” and “diplomacy by press release” are sometimes used to describe her style. Neither phrase was found attached to her in any 2026 source; they are this dossier's characterisation of a documented pattern, and they are labelled as such rather than presented as something anyone said. Similarly, the most-quoted insult of the year — that she privately called her own institutional rival a dictator — is one anonymous official's account of a private remark, reported second-hand. It is not her words on the record and this dossier does not treat it as evidence about her positions, which is the only subject here.

What can and cannot be concluded

What the record supports is a pattern, not a motive. Announcing first can be a deliberate ratchet: it shifts the terms of debate, forces capitals to respond to something concrete, and converts a private objection into a public one that carries a cost. Judged that way, a withdrawn initiative is not necessarily a defeat — but it is also not evidence that the strategy is working.

How verdicts were assigned

A claim is marked supported when independent published data confirms its substance. Mixed when the direction is right but the scale, definition or time window is doing unacknowledged work. Contested when credible institutions disagree about the facts themselves. Not verifiable when the statement was private or second-hand. No verdict is assigned to a political preference.

Known limits

Quotes delivered in other languages are translations and are marked as such. Several positions were developed across many speeches rather than in a single sentence, so the pull-quotes are representative rather than exhaustive. Trade, spending and asset figures are rounded orders of magnitude; where a precise series exists it is named in the caption.

Deliberate exclusions

Personal life, family, education, and biography. Domestic party politics and the internal affairs of any government. Speculation about motive. Anything that would be better served by a biography rather than by an audit of positions.

Corrections

This is a static document. If a cited figure is superseded by later reporting, the entry should be read against the newer source, and the chart caption revised to match.
